CHAPTER ONE. The Lighthouse Keeper.

The keeper climbed the same one hundred and twelve steps every evening, and every evening he counted them, not because he feared losing count but because the counting itself had become a kind of company. The lamp at the top was older than he was. It had outlasted three storms that had names and hundreds that did not.

On the night this story begins, a small boat appeared where no boat should be, tacking against a wind that wanted nothing to do with it. The keeper watched it for a long moment, then did the only thing a keeper can do. He made the light brighter.

CHAPTER TWO. The Visitor.

The sailor who came ashore was young and entirely unbothered by having nearly drowned. She asked for tea as if the sea had merely been a long queue she had finally gotten through. The keeper made the tea, because lighthouse keepers are, above all, practical people.

She explained that she was mapping the coastline, every rock and current of it, so that fewer boats would end the way hers nearly had. The keeper considered this. In forty years he had saved perhaps a dozen ships with his lamp. A good map, he thought, might save a thousand.

CHAPTER THREE. The Trade.

So they made a trade. She stayed a season and learned the water from the one man who knew it best, and he learned that his hundred and twelve steps could lead somewhere other than up. When she left, the map she carried had his handwriting in its margins.

The lamp still turns. The map is still used. And if you listen to this story again tomorrow, the lighthouse will still be exactly where you left it, which is the entire point of lighthouses, and perhaps of stories as well.

THE END.
